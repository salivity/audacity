How to Double Vocals in Audacity with Delay and EQ

Doubling a vocal track creates a thicker, more professional sound by mimicking the natural nuances of two separate takes. In Audacity, you can emulate this studio technique on a single recording by duplicating your lead vocal, applying a micro-delay to create subtle time separation, and using equalization (EQ) to shape the tonal balance so the duplicate complements the original without causing phase cancellation or muddiness.

Step 1: Duplicate the Lead Vocal Track

  1. Open your project in Audacity and select the original vocal track.
  2. Press Ctrl + D (or Cmd + D on macOS) to duplicate the track.
  3. Rename the original track to "Lead Vocal" and the new track to "Vocal Double" using the dropdown menu on each track header.

Step 2: Apply Subtle Delay to the Duplicate

A standard vocal double relies on a short delay—typically between 20 and 40 milliseconds—to create separation without sounding like an echo.

  1. Select only the "Vocal Double" track.
  2. Go to Effect > Delay and Reverb > Delay.
  3. Configure the settings:
    • Delay level per echo: -100 dB (or set the number of echoes to 1 to ensure a single repeat).
    • Delay time: Set between 0.02 and 0.04 seconds (20 to 40 ms).
    • Pitch change effect: None.
  4. Alternatively, use the Time Shift Tool (or hold down your mouse and drag the clip in Audacity 3.x+) to physically nudge the duplicate track slightly to the right by 20 to 30 milliseconds.

Step 3: EQ the Doubled Track

If both tracks have the exact same frequency profile, they will mask each other and create a hollow, phasey tone. Carving space with an equalizer allows the original vocal to stay clear and upfront.

  1. Select the "Vocal Double" track.
  2. Go to Effect > EQ and Filters > Filter Curve EQ.
  3. Apply a High-Pass Filter: Roll off frequencies below 150 Hz to eliminate low-end rumble and muddiness.
  4. Apply a Low-Pass Filter: Tame harsh sibilance by gently rolling off high frequencies above 8 kHz to 10 kHz.
  5. Cut the midrange: Reduce frequencies between 1 kHz and 3 kHz by 2 to 4 dB. This pushes the doubled vocal backward in the mix, leaving room for the lead vocal to cut through.

Step 4: Pan and Balance the Mix

  1. Pan the tracks: Keep the "Lead Vocal" panned to the center. Pan the "Vocal Double" slightly to one side (e.g., 20% to 30% Left or Right) to enhance the stereo width.
  2. Adjust volume levels: Lower the gain slider on the "Vocal Double" track by -6 dB to -10 dB. The doubled vocal should not be distinctly audible as a separate performance; instead, you should only notice its absence when the track is muted.
