Audacity Graphic EQ vs Filter Curve EQ Explained

Audacity provides two distinct equalization tools—Graphic EQ and Filter Curve EQ—to shape audio frequency content. While both tools allow you to boost or cut frequencies to alter the tonal balance of a recording, they differ fundamentally in their user interfaces, precision, and intended workflows. This article breaks down how each effect operates, their core differences, and how to choose the right one for your audio editing tasks.

Graphic EQ

Graphic EQ uses a familiar slider-based interface modeled after traditional hardware equalizers.

  • Fixed Frequency Bands: The tool provides a set number of vertical faders assigned to predetermined frequency bands (typically standard 31-band third-octave intervals).
  • Slider Controls: Adjusting the sliders boosts or cuts the volume of specific frequency ranges by a set decibel amount.
  • Ease of Use: It is intuitive for users who want to quickly warm up a voice, reduce harshness, or brighten a dull track without needing to know exact numerical frequencies.

Because the frequency centers are fixed, Graphic EQ is primarily suited for broad, musical tonal adjustments rather than precise corrections.

Filter Curve EQ

Filter Curve EQ uses a flexible graph-based interface where you shape a continuous frequency response curve.

  • Freeform Nodes: Instead of fixed sliders, you click anywhere on the graph to create points (nodes) at any exact frequency between 20 Hz and 20,000 Hz.
  • Custom Curves: You can drag nodes to create custom slopes, sharp notches, broad plateaus, or steep cutoffs.
  • Surgical Precision: This tool allows you to isolate and eliminate narrow problem frequencies, such as electrical hums (e.g., 50 Hz or 60 Hz) or sharp vocal sibilance, without affecting neighboring frequencies.

Filter Curve EQ is the preferred tool for high-pass or low-pass filtering and surgical audio repair.

Key Differences

  • Control Method: Graphic EQ adjusts levels via fixed vertical faders. Filter Curve EQ adjusts levels by plotting and moving points along a continuous 2D frequency graph.
  • Precision: Graphic EQ adjusts preset frequency bands with fixed bandwidths. Filter Curve EQ lets you define the exact frequency, cut depth, and curve shape.
  • Presets and Flexibility: While both allow saving presets, Filter Curve EQ presets can accommodate complex, asymmetric curves that cannot be replicated with Graphic EQ sliders.
  • Workflow Speed: Graphic EQ is faster for broad tonal sweetening. Filter Curve EQ requires more deliberate placement but provides superior control over problem audio.

Which One Should You Use?

Choose Graphic EQ when you need quick, intuitive tonal balance—such as adding low-end warmth to a podcast voice or taming overall high-end harshness.

Choose Filter Curve EQ when you need corrective audio work, such as rolling off rumble below 80 Hz, notching out specific resonance spikes, or crafting detailed, multi-point frequency profiles.
