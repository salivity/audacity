Create Vinyl Crackle and Tape Saturation in Audacity

This guide details how to recreate the vintage warmth of analog audio by generating synthetic vinyl crackle and tape saturation directly inside Audacity. Without needing third-party commercial plugins, you can use Audacity’s native noise generation, equalization, distortion, and modulation tools to simulate the imperfections of analog media. Following these steps will allow you to add authentic lo-fi texture, harmonic warmth, and subtle pitch instability to any audio project.

Part 1: How to Generate Synthetic Vinyl Crackle

Vinyl record surface noise consists of two main elements: steady low-level hiss and irregular, sharp clicks and pops. You can synthesize both from scratch.

Step 1: Generate the Base Hiss

  1. Open Audacity and create a new mono track by going to Tracks > Add New > Mono Track.
  2. Go to Generate > Noise....
  3. Set the noise type to Pink and set the amplitude to 0.05 to keep it subtle. Set the duration to match your project length, then click Apply.
  4. To shape the hiss into an authentic vinyl frequency profile, open Effect > EQ and Filters > Filter Curve EQ.
  5. Apply a high-pass filter cutting off frequencies below 200 Hz and a low-pass filter gently rolling off frequencies above 7,000 Hz.

Step 2: Create the Pops and Clicks

  1. Create a second new mono track (Tracks > Add New > Mono Track).
  2. Go to Generate > Noise... and select White noise at an amplitude of 0.8.
  3. Highlight this white noise track and select Effect > Volume and Compression > Noise Gate.
  4. Set the Gate threshold very high (around -2 dB to -4 dB) and set the Attack and Release times to their absolute minimums (1 ms). This removes almost all the audio, leaving behind random, erratic spikes of noise that mimic dust pops.
  5. Soften the harshness of the clicks by opening Effect > EQ and Filters > Low-Pass Filter and setting the cutoff frequency to 4,000 Hz with a 12 dB rolloff.

Step 3: Blend and Mix

  1. Pan the hiss slightly to one side and the pops slightly to the other if you prefer a wider field, or keep them centered for a vintage mono record effect.
  2. Lower the overall track sliders of both noise tracks so they sit unobtrusively beneath your main mix.

Part 2: How to Create Tape Saturation

Analog tape saturation naturally compresses dynamic peaks, rolls off extreme frequency extremes, and adds subtle harmonic distortion along with slight pitch variations (flutter).

Step 1: Shape the Frequency Curve

  1. Select the track you want to treat with tape saturation.
  2. Go to Effect > EQ and Filters > Filter Curve EQ.
  3. Replicate tape's frequency limits:
    • Roll off extreme low-end rumble with a gentle slope starting below 35 Hz.
    • Add a subtle wide boost (+1 dB to +2 dB) around 100 Hz – 250 Hz for low-end warmth.
    • Roll off the extreme high-end fizz starting around 14,000 Hz – 16,000 Hz.
  4. Click Apply.

Step 2: Add Harmonic Distortion (Drive)

  1. With the track still selected, go to Effect > Distortion and Modulation > Distortion.
  2. Set the Distortion Type dropdown to Soft Clipping or Tape Emulation (if available in your version).
  3. Adjust the Drive control to between 2 and 5. This rounds off sharp transient peaks and introduces the odd-order harmonics characteristic of overdriven tape.
  4. Keep the Output Level around -1 dB to prevent digital clipping (0 dBFS overs).
  5. Click Apply.

Step 3: Add Dynamic Smoothing (Tape Compression)

  1. Go to Effect > Volume and Compression > Compressor.
  2. Set the Threshold to roughly -12 dB, the Ratio to 2:1, and the Attack Time to around 20 ms.
  3. Set the Release Time to around 100 ms.
  4. Click Apply. This glues the audio together, mimicking how magnetic tape gently smooths loud passages.

Step 4: Add Tape Wow and Flutter (Optional)

  1. Go to Effect > Distortion and Modulation > Vibrato.
  2. Set the Frequency to roughly 1 Hz to 3 Hz (simulating slow motor speed irregularities).
  3. Set the Depth to a very low value, between 0.02 and 0.05.
  4. Click Apply. A microscopic amount gives the impression of an aging tape motor without making the pitch sound audibly out of tune.
