Remove Hiss in Audacity with a Low-Pass Filter

Vintage recordings frequently suffer from tape hiss, vinyl surface noise, and electrical interference that obscure the original audio. Audacity’s Low-Pass Filter eliminates this unwanted high-frequency noise by allowing audio signals below a specified cutoff frequency to pass through while systematically attenuating (quieting) frequencies above that threshold. By isolating the upper frequency spectrum where tape and mechanical hiss predominantly reside, the filter restores clarity to older recordings without substantially altering the core vocals or instruments underneath.

Understanding High-Frequency Hiss in Vintage Audio

Vintage recordings—such as analog cassette tapes, vinyl records, and shellac 78s—inherently contain noise generated by the recording medium itself. Tape hiss, for example, is caused by the magnetic particles coating the tape, which emit continuous white or pink noise typically centered above 5,000 Hz to 8,000 Hz. Because human speech fundamentals and most musical instruments produce their primary energy in the low and mid-ranges, this high-frequency hiss can often be isolated and suppressed without destroying the underlying performance.

How the Low-Pass Filter Operates

A low-pass filter does not create a sudden, total silence at the chosen cutoff mark; instead, it creates a progressive slope of attenuation. It relies on two primary parameters to clean audio:

  • Cutoff Frequency (Hz): The frequency at which the filter begins attenuating the audio signal. Frequencies below this point remain untouched, while frequencies above it are gradually decreased in volume.
  • Rolloff (dB per octave): The steepness of the attenuation curve. A gentle rolloff (e.g., 6 dB or 12 dB per octave) softly fades out the high frequencies, creating a natural transition. A steeper rolloff (e.g., 24 dB, 36 dB, or 48 dB per octave) aggressively cuts high-frequency content immediately past the cutoff point.

By setting the cutoff frequency just above the highest relevant harmonics of the original performance, the filter suppresses the steady hiss that occupies the upper end of the spectrum.

Applying the Filter in Audacity

  1. Analyze the Audio: Select a section of the track and open the Plot Spectrum tool (under Analyze) to observe where the audio's intended frequencies taper off and where only hiss remains.
  2. Open the Effect: Highlight the desired audio track, navigate to the Effect menu, select EQ and Filters, and choose Low-Pass Filter.
  3. Configure Cutoff Frequency: For human speech or degraded acoustic recordings, a cutoff between 5,000 Hz and 7,000 Hz often eliminates substantial noise. For music containing cymbals or bright brass, a higher threshold between 8,000 Hz and 10,000 Hz may be required to retain musical brightness.
  4. Set the Rolloff: Start with a moderate slope, such as 12 dB or 24 dB per octave.
  5. Preview and Apply: Use the Preview button to ensure the track does not sound overly muffled, adjust as necessary, and click Apply.

Balancing Noise Reduction and Audio Fidelity

The primary challenge when using a low-pass filter is preventing the audio from sounding dull or "underwater." If the cutoff is placed too low, critical harmonic frequencies, vocal sibilance ("s" and "t" sounds), and percussion attack are stripped away alongside the hiss. Effective vintage audio restoration requires finding the highest possible cutoff frequency that still provides an acceptable reduction in background hiss.
