How to Use Audacity Noise Gate to Silence Room Tone

A Noise Gate in Audacity cleans up spoken recordings by automatically attenuating ambient room tone, microphone hiss, and low-level background noise whenever a speaker pauses. Instead of manually editing out the silence between sentences, the effect monitors the volume of the audio track and clamps down on the signal whenever it drops below a user-defined threshold. When the speaker begins talking again, the gate instantly opens, allowing the voice to pass through unaltered. This creates a clean, professional sound for podcasts, voiceovers, and dialogue tracks while saving significant editing time.

How the Noise Gate Mechanism Works

A noise gate functions like an automated volume fader that operates based on signal amplitude:

  • Open State: When the speaker talks, the signal level exceeds the set threshold. The gate opens, and the voice passes through completely unaffected.
  • Closed State: During natural pauses, breaths, or hesitations, the signal drops below the threshold. The gate closes, reducing the output volume of the room tone to a specified level or muting it entirely.

Because room tone is consistent and lower in volume than spoken words, setting the threshold between the ambient noise floor and the softest speech sounds ensures that only the pauses are silenced.

Essential Noise Gate Settings in Audacity

To configure the Noise Gate effectively in Audacity (found under Effect > Noise Removal and Repair > Noise Gate), you need to balance several primary controls:

  • Gate Threshold (dB): The volume cutoff point. Any audio quieter than this decibel level triggers the gate to close. If your room tone sits around -45 dB and your speech peaks around -12 dB, a threshold between -35 dB and -40 dB is typically ideal.
  • Level Reduction (dB): How much the volume is lowered when the gate is closed. Setting this to -100 dB produces total digital silence. Setting it to a subtler level, such as -12 dB to -18 dB, reduces the ambient tone significantly without creating an unnatural vacuum between words.
  • Attack Time (ms): How quickly the gate opens when speech resumes. A fast attack (typically 10 to 50 milliseconds) prevents the gate from clipping the first syllable or consonant of a word.
  • Hold Time (ms): How long the gate stays fully open after the signal drops below the threshold. This prevents the gate from stuttering or closing prematurely during brief micro-pauses between words.
  • Decay (Release) Time (ms): How smoothly the gate fades out once the hold time ends. A moderate release (between 100 and 250 milliseconds) ensures natural tail-offs on trailing words rather than harsh, abrupt cutoffs.

Step-by-Step Configuration

  1. Measure Your Noise Floor: Select a section of your recording that contains only room tone (a spoken pause). Look at Audacity’s playback meter to determine its decibel level (e.g., -48 dB).
  2. Select Your Audio: Highlight the entire track or the dialogue section you want to process.
  3. Open the Effect: Navigate to Effect > Noise Removal and Repair > Noise Gate.
  4. Set the Threshold: Choose a threshold roughly 6 to 10 dB higher than your measured room tone, but well below your quietest spoken word.
  5. Adjust Timing and Reduction: Set the Level Reduction to roughly -18 dB for a natural reduction, or lower for complete silence. Set Attack to around 20 ms and Decay to around 150 ms.
  6. Preview and Apply: Use the Preview button to ensure the beginnings and endings of words are not cut off. Click Apply once the balance sounds natural.

Best Practices for Spoken Dialogue

Total digital silence can sound jarring to listeners if the transition between speech and pauses is too extreme—an artifact often referred to as "breathing" or "pumping." To prevent this, apply moderate attenuation rather than complete muting, allowing a faint trace of room tone to remain. Additionally, always verify that quiet vocal dynamics, such as whispers or trailing consonants, do not fall below the threshold and become accidentally muted.
